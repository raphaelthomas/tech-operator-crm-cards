# Plan Continuation Bias ➡️

## What it is

Continuing with the original plan despite cues that suggest changing it. Known in aviation
as **get-there-itis** - a mindset to go, or to carry on going.

It is not a failure to notice - the cues are usually available, and often seen. What fails
is abandoning a plan already in motion, and the pull strengthens the closer you get to
finishing.

## Origin & evidence

Orasanu, Martin and Davison at NASA-Ames re-examined an NTSB set of **37 accidents** in
which crew behaviour contributed, containing **51 tactical decision errors**. Sorting them
into plan-continuation and everything else:

- **38 of 51 - almost 75% - were plan-continuation errors.**
- The two commonest were failing to perform a missed approach, and failing to go around in
  questionable weather or with an unstabilised approach.
- **Two thirds of the plan-continuation errors were omissions** - failures to take a
  required action, rather than wrong actions taken.

> Orasanu, J., Martin, L. & Davison, J. (2001). *Cognitive and contextual factors in
> aviation accidents: Decision errors.* In Salas, E. & Klein, G. (eds), Linking Expertise
> and Naturalistic Decision Making. Mahwah, NJ: Lawrence Erlbaum, pp. 209–225.
> https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20020063485

The paper's account of *why* is the useful part. Two conditions induce it: **ambiguous,
dynamically changing cues** - conditions that deteriorate gradually rather than tripping a
clear threshold - and **organisational or social goal conflicts**, the pressure to arrive,
to finish, to not be the one who called it off. The errors are then mediated by
underestimating the risk, overestimating your ability to handle it, and not projecting the
consequences of continuing.

The same data holds the opposite error too: 13 cases where crews took unnecessary
out-of-the-ordinary actions, including *rejecting a takeoff above V1*. Abandoning a plan at
the wrong moment is also an error - which is the argument for fixing the moment in advance
rather than by feel.

## How to use it

- **Pre-commit the abort condition** before you start - see
  [Pre-Committed Triggers](./pre-committed-triggers.md). This is the primary defence, and
  the bias is the reason it exists.
- **Say the goal conflict out loud.** "We want this done before the freeze" operates
  whether or not it is named; naming it lets someone else weigh it.
- **Treat gradual degradation as the danger case.** A cue that never crosses a threshold is
  the exact condition the research identifies.
- **Look for omissions.** Two thirds were things not done. Ask what step is being skipped
  because you are pressing on.
- **Apply the fresh-eyes test:** knowing what you know now, would you start this change
  today? If not, continuing needs a reason beyond having already begun.

## Worked example

A migration is forty minutes into a sixty-minute window. Replica lag has crept from two
seconds to nine in increments too small to trip the alert. Everyone can see it, nobody
calls it, each step looks survivable, and the freeze starts tomorrow. All three conditions
are present: ambiguous deteriorating cues, a goal conflict, and a plan most of the way
through.

## Limits

Naming a bias does not remove it, and this one operates hardest exactly when you are least
able to audit yourself. The evidence is also from flight crews rather than software
operators; what transfers is the mechanism and the conditions, not the rate. The defences
that work are external or decided in advance.
