# Fatigue and Performance ⏱️

## What it is

The evidence behind every time limit in these cards. Fatigue is a measurable decline in
cognitive performance on a predictable schedule — and it takes your judgement before it
takes your sense of having good judgement. That is why the thresholds are clock-based:
self-assessment is the first thing to go.

## Origin & evidence

**Hours awake.** Dawson and Reid kept subjects awake and compared their performance
against the same subjects dosed with alcohol. After 17 hours of sustained wakefulness,
cognitive psychomotor performance had fallen to the level produced by a blood alcohol
concentration of 0.05%. By roughly 24 hours it matched 0.10% — above the drink-driving
limit in most jurisdictions.

> Dawson, D. & Reid, K. (1997). *Fatigue, alcohol and performance impairment.*
> Nature 388:235. https://doi.org/10.1038/40775

**Hours on shift.** Folkard and Tucker pooled national accident statistics across
studies that corrected for exposure. Risk rises approximately exponentially with time on
shift: by the twelfth hour it is more than double the average of the first eight.

> Folkard, S. & Tucker, P. (2003). *Shift work, safety and productivity.*
> Occupational Medicine 53(2):95–101. https://doi.org/10.1093/occmed/kqg047

**Hours since a break.** In a plant giving a 15-minute break every two hours, injury risk
climbed almost linearly between breaks, roughly doubling by the last half-hour before the
next one. The effect held on both day and night shifts.

> Tucker, P., Folkard, S. & Macdonald, I. (2003). *Rest breaks and accident risk.*
> The Lancet 361(9358):680. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(03)12566-4

**Consecutive days.** The same review found risk accumulating across successive shifts:
about 6%, 17% and 36% higher on the second, third and fourth night shift. Day shifts
showed the same shape, smaller — about 2%, 7% and 17%.

**What it costs.** Landrigan and colleagues randomised intensive-care interns between a
traditional rota with shifts of 24 hours or more and one capped at 16. On the traditional
rota they made 36% more serious medical errors, and 5.6 times as many serious diagnostic
errors.

> Landrigan, C. P. et al. (2004). *Effect of reducing interns' work hours on serious
> medical errors in intensive care units.* New England Journal of Medicine 351:1838–1848.
> https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa041406

## How to use it

- **Break every two hours.** Not when you feel like it — the risk climb is steady and you
  will not notice it.
- **Hand off well before the twelfth hour.** Risk has more than doubled by then.
- **Past 17 hours awake, stop touching production.** You are operating at the
  drink-driving limit. Nobody would let you deploy drunk.
- **Count consecutive days, not just today.** The fourth night in a row carries its own
  penalty regardless of how rested you feel this morning.

## Worked example

Hour nine of an incident, third night on call, convinced you are close to a fix. Every
one of the three curves above is against you, and the state feels exactly like being fine.
That is the case the clock exists for.

## Limits

These are population statistics, not a prediction about you tonight, and none of the
studies were run on software operators. What transfers is the *shape*: accelerating risk
with hours awake, hours on task and consecutive days. Treat the numbers as thresholds for
handing over, not as a licence to run up to them.
